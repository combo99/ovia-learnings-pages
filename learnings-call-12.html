One problem. One mechanism. And show it on screen.

01

The three rules that decided this call

These showed up in every single batch — twice as the reason a winner won, twice as the reason a loser lost.

One big idea
you can point at
Batch#98 had insulin, GLP-1, serotonin and cravings all in the same ad. Nothing was strong enough to point to — and that is exactly where the belief gap opened up.
Promise cravings,
not the body
CB25 and CB22 stop at "your cravings will be gone" and women over 50 still buy. The prospect does the rest of the math herself.
Show it,
don't say it
The two top spenders, batch #88 and batch #100, do little more than show somebody physically refusing food.

🎯Why "cravings" outperforms "belly fat"

This was the sharpest insight of the call, and it explains the whole win/lose split.

  • These women have already tried eating less. They are 50+, they have starved themselves, and it did not burn the belly fat. So an ad that says "I have to remind myself to eat" and then promises a flat stomach is repeating the thing that already failed them.
  • Batch#98 opens on a flat stomach. The very first visual promises a visual before/after. That is a claim they have heard from every brand and disbelieved from every brand.
  • The winners promise only zero cravings. No weight-loss claim, no body claim. And it works on women over 50 and over 60 anyway — because they complete the chain: no cravings → I eat less → I lose it.

The rule: cravings and hunger are the only thing we promise in the lead. Physical results still appear — but as proof after the promise, never as the claim we hammer.

🧠If they can't picture it, it isn't a mechanism

Batch#102's hook was "you lost the insulin sensitivity she has." Nobody on the call could visualize it, and there was nothing on screen to help.

  • Compare it to what works. "Kneecap grinding." "Parasites." Emma's "poop paralysis." One phrase and you both see it and feel it.
  • The audience is barely aware anyway. Roughly a quarter of buyers have even heard of insulin resistance, per the post-purchase survey.
  • The fix is a rename, not an explanation. Something like "menopause belly" — a body part, plainly named, that a 55-year-old recognizes instantly.
  • Khush's framing: "Doctors call it insulin sensitivity. I call it ___." It signals you are one of them rather than a medical lecture.

The test: if you can't picture it while reading it, rename it before it ships.

02

The winning structure: SHOW → STARBUCKS → MECHANISM

Both winners run the same three beats in the same order. This is the skeleton to reuse.

1️⃣SHOW the not-hungry reveal

Open on the character physically refusing food — saying no to Chipotle, no to Bojangles, pushing away a donut. Not narrating it. Doing it, on camera.

→
Batch#98 never shows it. It only says she isn't hungry — which is precisely the claim they don't believe.
→
Bake it into the script too. "Look, I don't even want this donut. That's how well this works."

2️⃣Introduce it through the Starbucks cup

This is the beat the team keeps underrating. Ease was the most common reason buyers gave in the post-purchase survey — it tastes like a Starbucks drink, so trying it feels like nothing.

  • It creates a category of one. Another berberine supplement competes with a sophisticated market. The first-ever strawberry refresher that kills cravings competes with nobody.
  • Familiar plus novel. Even a 65-year-old who has never ordered one has heard of it — the ad borrows the familiarity and the craving twist supplies the novelty.
  • It conceals the sell. It doesn't read as being sold to; it reads as a drink you already know that happens to do something new.

Editors: use real Starbucks cups. A pink Starbucks cup in frame, or a Starbucks water cup they mix the packet into. Seeing it does more than saying it.

3️⃣Keep the mechanism stupid simple

In CB25 the mechanism is one ingredient — 5-HTP and serotonin — and nothing else. No bouncing between GLP-1, yerba mate and insulin.

→
The mechanism is a justification, not the pitch. It exists only to explain why the product works at all. The taste and the ease do the actual selling.
→
Leo's open experiment: swap the order — Starbucks intro first, mechanism after — and see whether framing everything as "just a Starbucks drink" lifts conversion.

🎭The fourth beat nobody named: open on drama

Both winners start somewhere inherently negative, and that is what pulls people in.

  • CB22 opens on an accusation — "my girlfriend is starving herself" — and the boyfriend's line does the heavy lifting: "you're literally drinking that sugary ass drink, but you never want to eat anymore." Two polar opposites in one sentence.
  • The spiking concept works the same way. Spiking someone's drink reads as a bad thing. You don't explain why — the ambiguity is the curiosity.
  • Then it names itself in plain language: "this is literally my craving control." Not the product name. Not "a supplement." A name she made up that says what it does.

The long-term ask, from Leo, for every creative strategist: can we turn our skits into stories? Put the pain before it and the fallout in the middle. The brands printing right now don't feel like ads — they feel like stories you're already inside.

🔬How to build sub-avatars (Khush)

Worth its own block because the team was about to do it backwards.

→
Don't start with the avatar. Searching "gym girls" and "menopausal women" on Reddit just confirms the avatar you already picked. That's confirmation bias.
→
Start with the problem. Search binge eating, binge eating disorder, emotional eater. Read the threads. The sub-avatars surface on their own — women in their 50s and women in their 20s saying the same thing.
→
Stay broad. The problem is constant; only how it manifests changes. A 20-year-old wants to look good for her boyfriend. A 50-year-old cares about blood sugar and diabetes.
03

Batch teardowns

Two losers first, because they define the problem — then the two winners that solve it. Spend on the losers was low, so treat the numbers as directional rather than conclusive.

🧩Batch#98 — iteration of CB27-C lost ~$70 over ~10 days high hook rate, high CTR, no sales

What went wrong
  • A conversion problem, not an attention problem. Hook rate and CTR were both strong. They clicked through the listicle, reached the product page, and didn't buy — which means belief, not interest.
  • No single big idea. Little bits of insulin, GLP-1, serotonin and cravings, none strong enough to point at. The "coherent mechanism" ended up being a mix of everything.
  • It promised the body, not the cravings. First visual is a flat stomach. To a 50+ woman who has already starved herself, that is the claim that already failed.
  • It never shows the not-hungry reveal. It states it and moves on.
What to keep
  • The original's GLP-1 comparison. That is what gave the parent ad authority with the older audience in the first place.
  • The dress-too-small proof beat — it's the same device CB25 uses, and there it works.
Iterate
→
Test the insulin mechanism — but don't name it. "Your food is being stored as fat, and that's why you're exhausted." Then confirm with symptoms. Previous insulin ads failed because the explanation got complicated.
→
Rewrite in 50-year-old register. Facebook group instead of TikTok. Mei has one running.

🧩Batch#102 — the insulin-sensitivity hook lost barely spent low hook rate AND low hold rate

Modelled on a TikTok skincare video where the creator names a root cause the viewer had never considered. The structure was right; the root cause was unpicturable.

What went wrong
  • The hook can't be visualized. "You lost the insulin sensitivity she has." Kay replayed it three times and still didn't get it — and nothing on screen helps.
  • The root cause is too abstract. The inspiration video pointed at something you can see on a face. Insulin resistance is invisible.
  • It dies in the first 15 seconds. Meta never gives it a chance to spend, because the knife never gets twisted at the open.
  • The voice is flat throughout. No emotion in the read and none in the copy — it just states things plainly.
What to keep
  • The root-cause-reveal structure itself. It's the parasite playbook, and the parasite playbook works.
  • The practitioner-authority device — "she exposed that 82% of women over 40..." — is a solid statistic-that-flips-it beat.
Iterate
→
Rename the mechanism, then rebuild the hook around it. Visual and instantly understandable — a body part, not a biomarker.
→
Restructure: tease the root cause → previously tried solutions with symptoms → the statistic that flips it → more symptoms to confirm it.
→
Add the B-roll. Utkarsh's idea: a Pixar-style side animation of food traveling down the food pipe and converting straight into fat.
→
Symptoms callout version. Kay is testing leading with a symptom they already have, then funneling into the mechanism.

🏆CB25 — the 5-HTP / Starbucks talking head breakthrough winner old ad account

The cleanest example of the full structure. Leads with proof, introduces through Starbucks, one mechanism, then contrasts pain against ease.

Why it won
  • Proof before product. "I literally have to force myself to eat after taking this." Then: "I had to get my brother's wedding dress altered because it's now too big on me. But it fit two weeks ago."
  • It's not just proof — it's "it worked too well." A copywriting device: the product didn't merely work, it overshot. That's far more believable than a clean success story.
  • One mechanism only. 5-HTP raises serotonin, low serotonin drives mood cravings. Named once, explained in two lines, dropped.
  • Introduced through a Starbucks cup. "Just grab a little Starbucks water... my fun little pinkity drinkity."
  • Pain initiation, then ease. "Summer's coming and you don't want to be insecure in a bikini" — set directly against "I just keep one of these little packets in my purse." The effort gap does the closing.
  • The CTA is the whole big idea in one line: "a fun drink, but also control the cravings."
The one real flaw
  • She opens holding the product. No ad has ever cleared roughly a 35% hook rate when the creator starts with the jar in hand — people read "I'm being sold something" and scroll. Easiest possible iteration.
Iterate
→
New hook, no jar in frame. Mei already has this one running.
→
Make the avatar older. Same script, older creator. A big share of buyers are 60–70, not 50.
→
Push the "worked too well" beat further. "I had to stop drinking this because I lost so much weight." "My mom started calling me sticks." An ER visit as the hook — then tune it down to the believable version.
→
Show her not being hungry. Squeeze a burger or a donut into frame and have her refuse it.
→
Horizontal scale by avatar. Same script, gym-girl version, changed only for avatar congruency.

Worth noting: this script is structurally very close to batch #42. The old #42 iterations may have failed because each one added something. The move is to take things out, not put things in.

🏆CB22 — the skit breakthrough winner old ad account no major weaknesses

She says no to Chipotle, no to Bojangles, gets in the car, the boyfriend confronts her — and it turns out to be a Starbucks drink. Same three beats as CB25, wrapped in drama.

Why it won
  • It shows the not-hungry reveal first. Refusing junk food, on camera, before anything is explained.
  • One problem, one mechanism, super simple. Nothing else in the ad.
  • It opens negative. "My girlfriend is starving herself." The audience is pulled in by something that looks like it's going badly.
  • The contradiction is stated out loud. "You're literally drinking that sugary ass drink, but you never want to eat anymore" — the ad sets up its own paradox in the first breath.
  • "This is literally my craving control." She never says the product name. She gives it a function name, and that name is the whole positioning.
Iterate
→
Make everyone older. Don't change the script. Same ad, older people, different locations.
→
Find new ways to refuse the food. This is the main axis of iteration — a family gathering where she won't eat with his family plays as real drama before the reveal.
→
Swap the mechanism freely. Serotonin → GLP-1 activation would probably still work. The intro sequence is what carries it, not the science.
→
Keep the spiking ambiguous. Never explain why the drink is being spiked. Not knowing whether it's a prank or a kindness is the curiosity engine.
→
Editors: completely different visuals. See the action items — this is the one that decides whether Meta spends.

Concepts up for grabs

Everything the call agreed was worth making. Unclaimed ones are open — take one.

🍸The spiked-drink family from CB22

All variations on one beat: someone slips the drink to someone else, and the food refusal that follows is the payoff.

  • Indecisive girlfriend. She can't decide where to eat, he hands her the drink first — and then she doesn't want to go anywhere.
  • Junk-food girlfriend. He spikes her drink in the car before the restaurant; by the time they sit down she wants nothing.
  • Thanksgiving grandma. Her drink gets spiked, the turkey comes out, she doesn't want any of it.
  • The bride. Someone spikes the bride's drink and she can't eat her own wedding cake.
  • The ex-husband. He turns up at the wedding and spikes her drink. The most controversial version of the beat.
  • Reverse it — the boyfriend secretly drinking hers. He has no idea that's why he isn't hungry; she catches him. Doubles as a proof element. Mei — in progress, coupled with the nana character
  • Boyfriend never eats with his girlfriend, who loves to eat.
  • Family gathering. She won't eat with his family, it reads as something horrible, and it turns out she's just drinking this.
