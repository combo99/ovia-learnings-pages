Learnings Call #14

Two breakthrough winners in one week — and both of them sell the same thing: how easy it is. The mechanism came second.

4 batches reviewed · 2 breakthrough winners, 1 KPI winner, 1 loser · $17.8k combined spend · Aug 18, 2026

🧩 Open the Miro board

✂️ For strategists and editors — focused, do-this-now takeaways
It's a strawberry refresher that makes you lose weight.

Batch 128 won without ever saying she wasn't hungry. Batch 136 won with a mechanism you could explain to a child. The casing and the ease are doing the heavy lifting — the mechanism is the thing we get to swap.

01

What showed up in every batch

Three things repeated across all four teardowns. If you only take three things into this week's scripts and cuts, take these.

Lead with the
refresher
Both winners frame the product as a strawberry refresher in the opening seconds. Batch 126 holds it until a minute in; Batch 143 never uses it at all. That framing is the single biggest pattern in what's working.
Contrast the
effort
Not the pain — the effort. "You can kill yourself at the gym, or you can drink this." Batch 128 is 100% high valence: no knife-twisting, no urgency, and it still crushed.
Make her
say yes
Batch 136 asks three symptom questions before it explains anything. She qualifies herself instead of getting diagnosed at — which is what keeps a symptoms callout from feeling like an attack.

🔁The mechanism is a variable, not a constant

  • Match the mechanism to what she already believes. Serotonin for the women who say "my brain won't shut up about food." The fullness signal for the women who say "I can't stop eating." Metabolism for the women who think theirs is broken.
  • Batch 136 never mentions metabolism at all — it goes berberine for the fullness signal, 5-HTP for serotonin, chromium for blood sugar, yerba mate for energy. Batch 128 barely explains anything. Both are winners.
  • Plain beats deep. "You're fighting your body when you should be working with it" outperforms an insulin or cortisol lecture. Don't stack mechanisms.
  • Never say you don't understand it. "I don't fully understand it, it just does something in your brain" hands the reader a reason not to buy. One clean sentence instead.

The bet this opens up: if the refresher casing is what converts, we can bolt almost any mechanism onto it — "a strawberry refresher that balances your hormones," "…that lowers your cortisol" — and break into fresher sophistication levels. The ingredients support all of them.

👀Social proof: let someone else notice the change

  • Batch 128's whole engine is a second person. The boyfriend spots the loose jeans — she never announces her own weight loss. Batch 126 does the same with a doctor. Batch 143, the loser, has her narrate her own change with nobody to react to it.
  • Clothes are the measuring stick. Survey data is full of women measuring progress in band sizes and dresses that fit again, not pounds. "Your clothes are falling off you" lands harder than a number.
  • Keep the gym contrast soft. Batch 128 never says "don't go to the gym" — only "I thought you were killing yourself at the gym," which leaves room for the women who do walk or work out. Going harder than that narrows the audience.
02

The winning body structure

Pulled off the board as the pattern behind Batch 136 and Batch 128 — the order to build the next round of stories in.

🧱The puzzle pieces, in order

1
Show or say the result first. Batch 126 waits 10 seconds to say "17 pounds down" — the retention graph has already dropped by then.
2
Introduce the product as simple and easy — visually. Starbucks-style cup, packet covered so the shot doesn't read as an ad.
3
Hand the solution over from a third party. A nurse, a doctor, a friend, a comment she read. The winners never have her find it alone.
4
Run the symptom yes-chain. "Are you hungry an hour after you eat?" "Do you get shaky if you go too long without food?" "Do you think about food when you're not even hungry?"
5
Then explain the mechanism — in one plain line, matched to her belief.
6
Land the pain contrast at the end. Batch 136: three years of crying, and what fixed it is a packet in the bottom of a handbag.

Rejection is part of the structure. Batch 136 has her reject the drink first — "I gained 40 pounds, I don't need a pink drink, I need to stop eating." The skepticism is the reader's, said out loud before she can raise it herself.

🧊Lego pieces worth stealing this week

  • The price anchor. "The Starbucks one is like $7, this has to be insane." "Nope — 90 cents a packet."
  • The delivery objection. "I already tried berberine, it did nothing." "Capsules are the wrong delivery, most of it never gets absorbed — it has to go in as a drink."
  • The effort-equivalence hook. Drinking this is two hours on the treadmill, or ten miles on a bike (Infinity Hoop runs this shape constantly).
  • The open loop. Batch 136's story stays open until the very end — you don't find out what happens with the ex-husband until you've read the whole thing.
03

Batch teardowns

Four batches this week. Note Batch 143 only spent $52.68 — its numbers can't carry a decision, so treat that teardown as diagnosis, not verdict.

🧩Batch 128 — Boyfriend Notices GF's Weight Loss breakthrough winner $7,617 · ROAS 1.41

Camimode TikTok iteration · Skit · Mei (strategist & editor) · Broad woman avatar · Unaware · Top variant V1 · 7 days running

What made it work
  • The visuals carried it. Strawberry refresher in frame, the packet covered so it doesn't feel like an ad, the pants visibly loose.
  • Social proof as the engine. The boyfriend notices; she never sells herself.
  • The negative open. "Turn around" reads as concern — viewers expect bad news, then get "your clothes are falling off you."
  • Pure high valence. No urgency, no knife-twisting, no pain. It works entirely on desire.
  • Effort contrast. "I thought you were killing yourself at the gym." "No, I've just been drinking this."
What it never did
  • Never says she isn't hungry. Cravings get one sentence. The whole not-hungry angle we've been building on is simply absent — and it still won.
  • No before/after. The loose pants are the only visual proof.
  • No pain, no urgency. Which is an opportunity, not a flaw.
Iterate
→
Test the same skit at low valence. Open by calling out the problems, CB98-A style, and see if the mirror image also wins.
→
Pop a before image up mid-skit. One cut, to see whether it survives the skit format at all.
→
Swap the clothes, swap the people. Dresses instead of jeans; grandson and nana, girlfriend to girlfriend, a wedding.
→
Add the $7 Starbucks price anchor before the 90-cents reveal.

🧩Batch 136 — Husband Left Her For A 29 Year Old breakthrough winner $7,165 · ROAS 1.64 top spender

Lymphoria imitation · Long-form native / LFS · Mei (strategist) / Kay Bolla (editor) · 55+ avatar · Unaware · Top variant V3 · 4 days running

What made it work
  • The event is a real fear. Left at 52 for a 29-year-old — the story sits on something a lot of women in this avatar genuinely dread.
  • Insanely simple mechanism. Four ingredients, one job each, explained like she's a patient.
  • The three-question yes-chain. Symptom confirmation before any explanation. She self-qualifies rather than being diagnosed at.
  • Pain contrast. Three years of crying in parking garages, fixed by a packet in the bottom of a handbag.
  • Only the skeleton is borrowed. From Lymphoria we took the canonical shape — he cheats, she breaks, someone saves her, she gets her revenge. Everything inside it is ours.
What to tighten
  • Perimenopause may be dead weight. The symptoms do the work on their own — "I'd been avoiding mirrors," "my stomach sat on top of my jeans" — without needing to name a condition she may not identify with.
Iterate
→
Mass-produce the stories. Take the whole Lymphoria / Resilient story-ad list and rebuild each one on this exact puzzle-piece structure. This is the week's biggest volume lever.
→
Swap the lead event, keep everything else. Same intensity: fiancé dumped her two weeks before the wedding; he got her best friend pregnant; she was the fat mom at her son's wedding.
→
Strip the story entirely. Keep the yes-chain, the authority and the solution, and run it as a symptoms callout in the CB98-A direction.
→
Re-cut it as video. A yapper talking-head opening on "story time," and an AI UGC skit that plays the three questions as doctor-and-patient.

🧩Batch 126 — Nana's Doctor Visit Reveal KPI winner $2,994 · ROAS 1.01

B114 iteration · Skit · Mei (strategist & editor) · 50+ avatar · Unaware · Top variant V5 · 7 days running · Took ~5% of account spend at peak

What made it work
  • She was offered the shots and turned them down. Survey data is full of women who know about GLP-1s and don't want to be on one — and it plants the question "so what did you do instead?"
  • Third-party proof. The doctor's surprise does the believing for her.
Why it faded
  • The payoff is 10 seconds late. "17 pounds down" lands after the retention graph has already fallen off a cliff.
  • No refresher framing until the end. The "it's just a pink drink" casing shows up around the one-minute mark — by then it isn't doing any work.
  • A mechanism that undermines itself. "I don't fully understand it, it does something in your brain" — if she doesn't know why it works, why should the viewer buy it?
  • Captured attention, stopped converting. It held spend for a while and then couldn't convert new audience.
Iterate
→
Move the reveal to the first line. "The doctor thought the scale was broken."
→
Rebuild it on the Batch 128 structure. Result first, then product as easy and visible, then mechanism.
→
Put a Starbucks cup in the shot so the refresher idea is visual instead of stated.
→
Cut the "I don't understand how it works" line and replace it with one plain sentence.
→
Run it as a native. "My doctor offered me Ozempic — then called weeks later asking what I was doing."
→
New hooks off the clothes. The grandma complaining she keeps having to buy new clothes; or a negative-curiosity open where she's throwing a pile of clothes in the garbage.

🧩Batch 143 — Older Woman Story Native losing ad $52.68 · ROAS 2.62 low spend

Ideation from an organic Instagram video · Khush (strategist) / Utkarsh (editor) · Women 40+ peri/menopause · Problem aware · Top variant V3 · 10 days running

What went wrong
  • There's no story. No event, no turning point, nothing to feel. The one concrete moment — the clutch at the engagement party — sits at the very end.
  • No second character. She has the problem, finds the answer and explains it herself. Nobody hands her anything, so there's no authority to borrow belief from.
  • The refresher casing is missing. The product is never positioned as a strawberry refresher — the framing both winners are built on.
  • A defensive line lands too early. "I'm not going to tell you a drink did this, I still walk, I still get my protein" arrives before we've made the case for the product. That line only works at the end.
  • Awareness sits wrong. The open speaks to a woman who has tried everything; then it teaches insulin from scratch.
What to keep
  • The hook itself is fine. The problem starts after it — the drop-off is in the body, not the open.
  • The source instinct was right. It came from an organic Instagram video with real metrics and comments from women who thought it was real.
Iterate
→
Transplant the Batch 136 puzzle pieces wholesale. Inject a story into the hook — "my husband cheated on me" — then the authority, then the yes-chain.
→
Simplify the mechanism to one line. "You're fighting your body when you should be working with it" — no insulin lecture, no cortisol chain.
→
Test it with no native text at all. Video only, straight to the listicle, then the PDP.
→
Send it to a third-party page. An advertorial built off the Batch 136 story on a separate domain, with the ad's only job being to earn the click.

05

Still open

Genuine disagreements from the call. Don't write as if these are settled — they're what this week's tests are supposed to answer.

⚖️Two candidates for the load-bearing point

Setting the story aside, either the strawberry refresher casing is what converts, or it's the food noise and cravings material. We have ads supporting both: Batch 128 barely mentions cravings and wins on the casing; Batch 136 is heavily skewed to food noise and overeating and wins too. The answer changes how freely we swap mechanisms, so it's worth designing the next batches to actually separate the two.

⚖️How much education a solution-aware woman needs

One read on Batch 143: the open speaks to a woman who has tried everything, then lectures her about insulin as though she's never heard of it. The counter-read: solution aware is not the same as mechanism aware — she may know every diet and still not know why they failed, the same way someone can know biotin without knowing hair loss is hormonal. Unresolved.

⚖️Does a straight symptoms callout feel like an attack?

With food specifically, calling out her symptoms cold may read as an accusation and get scrolled past — which is the argument for the yes-chain doing the qualifying instead. But nobody's actually tested it in this market, and the CB98-A-style stripped version is going out this week partly to find out.

⚖️How big a promise the drink can carry

"You don't need the gym" is believable. "This replaces your meals" may be a bigger promise than the market will take — and the harder gym contrast risks the women who believe exercise genuinely matters. The OMAD and fasting concepts are the test of where that ceiling sits.

⚖️Whether a before image survives a skit

Popping a before shot up on screen is standard elsewhere and the reaction it earns is real — but a skit is supposed to feel like something that's happening, and a graphic overlay may break that. One cut, and let the market answer it.
