Cross-batch patterns

The three rules that came up in every single teardown this call. If you only change three things in your next script, change these.

The top winners are all iterations of data that already worked for us. Swiping other brands' accounts is why it took ~100 concepts to hit a winner — we can't isolate the variable on data we don't own.

Every time "GLP-1 activation" has worked, it landed after the proof, as the explanation for a result already on screen. As an opening claim it just asks the viewer to do the maths.

⏱Urgency to SOLVE the problem — not urgency to buy

Weight loss is numb pain. It's not a heart attack in two weeks; it's something you live with, so nothing forces the decision. Every ad that scales has borrowed a deadline from the calendar or from a life event.

  • Summer is CB46-B's built-in deadline. "Summer's in 90 days — if you start right now you'll finish right in time." That's what makes it easy to scale into Q1.
  • In the meantime, test holiday deadlines. Christmas is when everyone sees each other again and talks; New Year's is the other obvious one.
  • A wedding is the sharpest deadline we have. A fixed date by which you have to look good — that's why the Batch#28 wedding-dress story works.
  • Not "sells out in 24 hours." We are not creating urgency to buy the product. We are making the problem feel like it has to be fixed now.

Open question worth solving: the health route (perimenopause → visceral fat → cardiac risk) is being tested, but Khush's caution stands — every doctor already tells these women visceral fat is bad, so it's a message they've tuned out.

🎭Borrowed experience gets past beliefs that direct address triggers

  • Direct address tanks. Khush ran this on a men's ED supplement: creatives that said "your wife is going to leave you if you don't fix your plumbing down there" died. The same idea told as another man's story — a guy who lost his wife, warning others not to repeat his mistake — took off every time.
  • Our audience rejects the direct version too. Mei's Reddit research: these women do not accept "your marriage failed because of menopause." They frame it the other way — the husband is the problem, and menopause is what freed them from putting everyone above themselves.
  • The story lets them try the idea on. Watching it happen to someone else, they can think "this isn't one-to-one me, but I know this happens to women" — no defensiveness, and they still feel it.
  • Give the ending that lets them off the hook. The scaling story ads land on "it was never my fault — he just couldn't handle me." You can't open by implying she was ugly; you can weave the fear through and resolve it kindly.

Hypothesis, not settled: borrowed experience can overcome preexisting beliefs. There are clearly different segments here and it doesn't generalise cleanly — which is exactly why it gets tested as a story, not as a claim.

👑A strong authority figure — close to the character — introduces the product

In the story ads that scale, the product doesn't arrive from the narrator. Someone arrives: "my sister flew in from Brazil to help me after I got cheated on."

  • She's the living reference point. Beautiful, same family, same genetics — so the difference can't be dismissed as luck.
  • She's allowed to be blunt. Coming from someone close, the hard truths read as help instead of an attack.
  • She's the one who introduces the product. That's the moment the mechanism is allowed to show up.

⚖️Contested: how concealed should the product be?

This one is unresolved — it was argued both ways on the call and neither side has the data yet. Do not treat it as a rule.

  • Leo's side. In CB37-D the pack stays concealed in his hand, logo covered, and what you actually see is the finished strawberry refresher. In Batch#108 the product is held up beside her face from ~0:12 to the end, which reads as being sold to — and may be why Meta predicts lower conversions.
  • Mei's counter. Batch#114 showed the product in-face and still won. So "conceal it" can't be the whole story.
  • Leo's reply. Batch#88 and Batch#100 are currently outperforming Batch#114 — a reason to keep testing the concealed version, not a verdict against Batch#114.
  • The one nearby thing we do have data on: when the product is in the first frame, the hook rate has come back lower in our own tests. That's a separate, narrower finding — first frame, not "show it less overall."

How to run it: build the concealed variant as an A/B against the in-face version of the same script. That's the only way this gets answered.

02

Stories, belief & native ads

The back half of the call turned into a playbook for the native-ad batch that's going out next. Everything here is directly usable on the wedding-dress story.

🧠Belief structure: symptoms → root cause → more symptoms

When you introduce a mechanism the viewer hasn't heard of, the instinct is to explain it harder. The brands doing this well do the opposite.

  • Name three symptoms she recognises. Then: "these are all a sign of something deeper."
  • Name the root cause once. Don't lecture it.
  • Then go back to symptoms. "It's why you're exhausted constantly, it's why…" — a second set of hits builds belief far better than a detailed explanation. Getting her symptoms right is what makes her think these people know what they're talking about.

📖Native-ad craft — what makes them readable

  • Run one narrative loop under the whole thing. Open it early, don't resolve it until the end. The reference pattern: her husband was perfectly healthy and died of a heart attack — and now she's getting the exact same symptoms. You read on to find out whether she solves it in time.
  • Tease the visual before/after in the opening beat, then turn: "but it wasn't always like this — three months ago I was…"
  • Keep the before/after window on the edge of believability. 55–60 days. It should sound almost too good to be true, not impossible.
  • Cut hooks harder than feels comfortable. If a hook feels like too much, it usually isn't too much yet — take it one step further.
  • Study the format before writing. Rosabella and Rejuveen run strong native ads; Lymphoria has decent ones; Primal Queen has a stack of ads built on exactly the wedding-day narrative.

Why native first: it's the fastest way to test these story ideas without committing to a ten-minute long-form AI build — and the editing team is still being staffed up.

💍The wedding-dress story (Batch#28) — the story that's next

She'd been waiting years to get married, gained weight slowly, and by the wedding she was a size 14 — the seamstress couldn't zip her up. She finds a solution and ends up a size 4/6, and has to buy a new dress because the old one can't be taken in far enough.

  • The existing native version was never hyper-converting. The story is strong; the execution has room — more tension, a better narrative, the before/after teased up front.
  • Source the variations from r/weddingdrama. Sort the sub by engagement and the top posts hand you the hooks — the ones you read and immediately need to know what happened.
  • Age it up for the older avatar. Same deadline, different owner: it's her son's or her daughter's wedding, and the mother needs to fit into a dress.
03

Batch teardowns

Three of these four got little or no spend, so treat the diagnoses as hypotheses to test rather than settled causes — the sample is small and Meta's spend decision has many inputs. Batch#108 has also been forced into an ABO campaign to confirm.

🧩Batch#108 — husband confronts wife about Ozempic solid metrics low spend forced into ABO

An iteration in the CB37-D restaurant lineage (a dialogue that shows the product working) crossed with the confrontation hook from CB27-D, the girlfriend-confrontation top spender. Performance metrics and soft metrics are both good — Meta just won't push it.

Why we think Meta isn't pushing it
  • Avatar — Mei's hypothesis. The wife is attractive enough that this audience rolls its eyes: "well, obviously you feel like it changed your life." Every time we've run a very attractive woman it hasn't performed, including on the girlfriend confrontation.
  • No victim, no discomfort — Utkarsh. In Batch#88 the grandma looked like a victim, so people watched to see what would happen to her. Here the character looks clever and in control, so the hook is less uncomfortable and engagement runs lower.
  • Benefits stated, not shown — Utkarsh. Batch#88 played like an experiment and a personal discovery — she drank it, the cravings went away. This one has the woman listing benefits herself, with nothing achieved on screen.
  • The food is never pushed away. The plate sits beside her untouched, so it isn't clear she won't eat it. In CB37-D he physically shoves the food across the table and it comes straight back.
  • Product beside her face from ~0:12 onward. Held up next to her for the rest of the ad — see the contested section above; this is the batch that raised it.
  • Written English, not spoken English — Mei. The lines read as written copy rather than something a husband would actually say.
  • The confrontation doesn't carry. Ozempic gets named in the hook and never comes back, so the rest of the script isn't the same scene anymore.
What to keep
  • The confrontation hook. Catching someone — with a needle, or over the fact that they never want to eat and just keep drinking that Starbucks drink — is our best-performing hook family right now.
  • The dialogue format. A lot of our winning ads are dialogues.
  • The metrics. This is close to a winner, not a failure — that's why it's worth iterating rather than dropping.
Iterate
→
Put a needle in the hook. "I finally caught you — I know you're taking Ozempic" while holding the pen makes the accusation concrete instead of verbal.
→
Swap in a Starbucks cup. Same casing that carries CB46-B — familiar object, not a supplement.
→
Re-cast the avatar. A Batch#100-style avatar (not conventionally attractive, bigger) or a Batch#88-style grandma, against the current wife.
→
Grandson confronts Grandma. Same script, CB27-D / Batch#104 lineage. It's a bizarre pairing — that's the argument for testing it, not against.
→
Push the food away on camera. Make the not-hungry reveal a physical action, not an implication.
→
Weave the confrontation through the body. Keep referring back to the accusation so the whole ad stays one scene.
→
Rewrite for the mouth. Spoken English throughout.

🧬Batch#109 — the GLP-1 activation explainer low spend learning limited new concept

A brand-new concept for us: we took the organic format of a popular TikTok explainer — a researcher who talks straight to camera breaking things down — and used it to explain GLP-1 activation. The reason-why: berberine is how you activate GLP-1, but the mistake most people make is taking it without chromium, and you need chromium for the body to hear the GLP-1 signal.

Reference ad for the whistleblower iteration ↗
Why we think Meta isn't pushing it
  • The proof arrives far too late — Mei. "Activate your GLP-1" doesn't tell a woman what she gets. In every ad where the phrase has worked, it came after the result was shown, so the viewer concludes the results are because of GLP-1 activation. Strip the proof out of the opening and she can't reach that conclusion on her own.
  • Not enough of the audience can picture the outcome — Khush. Only people who already know what GLP-1 is and how you'd activate it can process the hook. When his own batches went learning limited it was usually because the language was too niche.
  • Counterpoint — Utkarsh. The audience isn't actually narrow: roughly 10 million Americans are on GLP-1 drugs, dealing with nausea, muscle loss and the rest, and actively looking for a natural way to activate it. The hook answers a question they're already asking — a benefit would tie it back to desire, but the reach isn't the ceiling.
  • Almost no future pacing. Nothing shows her what life looks like on the other side.
  • Almost no knife-twisting, no pain initiation. Weight loss is a low, numb, everyday pain — if the ad doesn't sharpen it, nothing makes her act today.
What to keep
  • GLP-1 activation is not the problem. CB46-B uses it and wins. The issue is placement, not the term.
  • It's our first deeper-mechanism batch that got spend and results. That alone makes the direction worth pushing.
  • The chromium reason-why. It gives someone who's already heard of berberine a brand-new reason to try this.
  • The organic explainer format. Native to the feed, and cheap to re-shoot.
Iterate
→
Recut, no new VO: move the "get results like this" beat at ~0:19 to the front. It becomes the first ~1.5 seconds; the rest of the ad is untouched. Fastest test on the board.
→
Push the visual before/after much harder. If it's going to open the ad it needs to make people say holy shit.
→
Put a benefit in the hook line itself. "Here's how to activate your GLP-1 naturally — and lose 2 pant sizes in 3 weeks."
→
Add future pacing, drop mechanism speak. Model it on the "women come to me at 40, 50 years old in tears because their faces have changed" opener from another brand's ad.
→
Whistleblower version — same body, new frame. She's inside an Ozempic facility: "my boss will fire me if he finds out, but there's a way to activate GLP-1 naturally that they don't tell you — so they can keep selling you these jabs."
→
Make it feel more forbidden. Lean the opening into what celebrities are doing and what you can do naturally instead, then hand off to the explanation.

🎞Batch#113 — animated GLP-1 mechanism breakdown no spend ABO forced · not profitable

Leo was confident going into this one. AI animation format, with the original organic inspiration being the Dr. Pickle-style educational account — the cursing and the skeleton visuals are native to that format, which is why the format is everywhere organically right now. V3 got the most delivery and still not enough; forced spend in the ABO wasn't profitable.

Why we think it's a loser
  • The mechanism is too complicated — it isn't one chain. The script jumps: GLP-1 activation in the hook, then 5-HTP raising low serotonin, then berberine, then yerba mate. Watching it, there's no single coherent story to follow.
  • One benefit is being sold as if it were the whole product — Utkarsh. The hook promises GLP-1 activation, then the body forces several unrelated parts in behind it. The inspiration ads say "here's what happens if you take this," then break down the ingredients inside it — the product is the container, not one of the benefits.
  • "GLP-1 activation" means something different to every viewer — Khush. Some hear the jab, some hear capsules. "Cutting mix" is instantly understood as a mix she stirs into something.
  • The mechanism of the problem is missing — Khush. "That's 5-HTP increasing your body's low serotonin" assumes she already knows serotonin is why she's craving. Tie the cause back to the exact word used earlier — cravings, food noise.
  • Mei's caution on that: "low serotonin from years of scrolling" is language that has already worked in a winning ad, so adding more explanation on top risks redundancy rather than clarity.
  • The format may not travel from men to women — Khush. The same script style is running for a men's muscle-supplement brand, and the language is built for men: "what the fuck happens to a menopausal woman," shirtless muscle visuals.
  • Utkarsh on that desire gap: women want to look good in clothes and pull attention, not carry visible muscle. Mei's counter: the muscles were being used as a visual hook, not as the desire being sold.
What to keep
  • The animation format itself. It's a proven organic scroll-stopper across a lot of accounts — the issue is the script inside it.
  • The ambition of stacking several ingredients. It's right; it just needs a casing to arrive in.
  • The anger. Cursing is native to this format — it just needs a legitimate target.
Iterate
→
Day 1 / Day 2 / Day 3 structure. Mei built this format for a previous brand and it's still spending. Day 1 your tummy starts going down — yerba mate. Day 2 your double chin — berberine. Each day pins one visible change to one ingredient, so future pacing and the ingredient stack land together. Then taste, then the rest of our winning structure.
→
Name the product, not the mechanism. "GLP-1 Strawberry Refresher" from Starbucks, or "the cutting mix" — a product-aware hook that collapses the mechanism into one thing.
→
Lead with the problem, and aim the anger at the doctors. "Why the hell do all these doctors keep telling women it's just age?" — it motivates the cursing and it's the stereotype the audience already lives in.
→
Alternative hook — Mei. "What happens to your body if you drink this strawberry drink?" over a visual crazy enough that it doesn't add up, so the question becomes how a strawberry drink could possibly do that.
→
Run symptoms → root cause → more symptoms. Confirm her experience instead of explaining the cause harder.
→
To study: brands that legitimately explain several pathways at once — e.g. the kids' health ads that say ADHD isn't a one-pathway problem, most parents only fix one of the four. That's how you stack mechanisms without confusion.

🏆CB46-B — the strawberry refresher winner winner ran a long time hook rate ~33%

An old winning ad, pulled back up because the idea inside it is the one we've under-used. Completely native content — she pours the drink into a Starbucks cup, and everything else follows from that one decision.

View the ad ↗
Why it won
  • The Starbucks cup is the casing. It's not the cup that converts — it's the idea around it. This is a drink that tastes like a strawberry refresher, something people already buy and already like, except it carries all these other benefits.
  • Which flips the offer. "It has all these benefits, and by the way it tastes like a refresher" is weak. "Stop drinking strawberry refreshers, drink this instead and get all of this" is the strong version.
  • The structure is insanely simple. 1) here are the results I got · 2) I got it from this drink · 3) this is how the drink works · 4) it tastes good · 5) it's cheap · 6) urgency — in this case, summer's coming.
  • Proof lands in the first sentence. She's holding the thing that works and telling you it worked before anything else happens.
  • The mechanisms are unbelievably simple and stay inside the casing. Speeds up the metabolism, 5-HTP for serotonin, and it has GLP-1 activation — stated, never explained. That may also be the way past berberine saturation: claim the activation without naming the ingredient everyone has heard of.
  • "I started drinking this shit way too soon." Broad enough that any viewer can read their own meaning into it, and curious enough that they want to know what "too soon" means.
The biggest remaining lever

Hook rate is only ~33% — much lower than that opening line deserves, and probably the single biggest thing we could improve on a proven winner. One likely cause: the product is visible in the first frame, and first-frame product has consistently come back with a lower hook rate in our tests.

Iterate
→
Age the avatar up. Identical script and structure, older woman. Cheapest possible iteration on a winner.
→
Negative-curiosity hook. "I regret drinking this — it's only March and I'm already ready to be in a bikini." Negative openings pull harder than positive ones online.
→
Double hook. Symptoms call-out first, then "I started drinking this shit way too soon" — symptoms call-outs have beaten this hook in other batches.
→
Open on the finished drink instead of the product. Have her hold up the end result in frame one and keep the packaging out of it.
→
Carry the urgency slot forward. Summer is the built-in deadline for Q1; between now and then, swap in a holiday or New Year's deadline in the same slot.
→
Keep the cup, centre the refresher. The strawberry refresher angle is the thing to carry into every other concept — it's the most under-used idea we own.
